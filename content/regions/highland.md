The Highland region covers everything north of an old excise boundary running roughly from
the Clyde to the Tay, minus the enclave that
[Speyside]({{< relref "/regions/speyside" >}}) carved out of it. It is the largest Scotch
region by area and by far the least useful as a flavour predictor.

## A region defined by exclusion

Highland whiskies range from the coastal and lightly smoky in the far north, through rich
sherried styles in the east, to soft and floral drams in the Perthshire hills. Island
distilleries outside [Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) — Skye, Orkney, Jura, Mull,
Arran — are folded into the Highland designation too, despite having little in common with
the mainland or with each other.

When a Highland whisky tastes a particular way, the reason is almost always the distillery's
own choices — still shape, cut points, cask policy — rather than anything regional. This is
the clearest case for navigating by [flavour]({{< relref "/flavours/vanilla" >}}) rather
than by map.

## What the label will not tell you

Because the region spans so much, a Highland bottling's age statement, strength and cask
policy carry far more information than its regional designation. A
[cask strength]({{< relref "/glossary/cask-strength" >}}) Highland
[single malt]({{< relref "/glossary/single-malt" >}}) from a [sherry]({{< relref "/flavours/sherry" >}}) cask
and a 40% ex-bourbon one share a region and essentially nothing else.

Where a Highland whisky is smoky, that is a distillery's deliberate choice rather than a
regional inheritance — see
[what makes a whisky peaty]({{< relref "/guides/what-makes-a-whisky-peaty" >}}).
