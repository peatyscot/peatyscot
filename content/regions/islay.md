Islay is a small island off Scotland's west coast, with a reputation out of all proportion
to its size. That reputation rests on a single flavour: one that most beginners describe
simply as smoke, and that most enthusiasts, in time, come to describe as seaweed, tar,
iodine and bonfire.

## Why Islay peat tastes different

To understand the whisky, you have to go to the bog.

Peat is compressed vegetation — the accumulated remains of whatever grew, died, and failed
to rot in a waterlogged place. And what grew there determines what the smoke will taste
like, thousands of years later, when it is finally burned.

Inland Highland peat is heavy with wood and heather. Islay's formed in coastal bogs, from
sphagnum moss, seaweed and salt-laden vegetation, and it burns to a smoke high in the
phenolic compounds that read as **medicinal** rather than **woody**. The TCP, the
antiseptic, the iodine — the notes that now define the island's style — were settled long
before there was a distillery here to use them.

The intensity of all this is measured as
[phenol ppm]({{< relref "/glossary/phenol-ppm" >}}), and it is quoted for the malted barley
rather than for the whisky in the glass.

## Islay is not uniformly peaty

The reputation obscures the range. [Lagavulin]({{< relref "/distilleries/lagavulin" >}}) and
[Ardbeg]({{< relref "/distilleries/ardbeg" >}}) sit within two miles of one another on the
island's south coast. Both distil heavily peated spirit. They taste markedly different —
Lagavulin denser and more sherried, Ardbeg brighter and more coastal.

Meanwhile, on the same island, Bruichladdich and Bunnahabhain built their identities on
**unpeated** spirit. An Islay label is a postcode. It is not a promise.
