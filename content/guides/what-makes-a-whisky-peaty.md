Peated whisky is made from the same three permitted ingredients as unpeated whisky: water,
malted barley and yeast. The difference is introduced once, early, in a single step — and
everything downstream can only modulate what happened there.

## The mechanism

It begins with the barley.

Harvested grain is steeped in water and allowed to germinate, converting its starch into
fermentable sugar. Left to itself, the growing plant would consume that sugar. So
germination is halted, and the grain is dried.

This is the moment — and the only moment — at which peat can enter the story.

Burn peat beneath the drying floor, and the smoke rises up through the damp malt. Phenolic
compounds in that smoke bind to the moist grain, and the moisture is essential, which is
precisely why peating is done at the start of drying, while the barley is still wet.

What binds there survives everything that follows. The mash. The ferment. The still. Years
in oak. **Peat cannot be added later.** A whisky is either made from peated malt or it is
not.

## Why coastal peat tastes medicinal

Peat is partially decomposed vegetation, and different bogs decomposed different plants.

Inland Highland bogs formed from heather, grasses and woody material, and they burn to a
smoke closer to a garden bonfire. Coastal [Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) bogs
formed from sphagnum moss and salt-laden, seaweed-rich vegetation, producing a phenol
profile that reads as [iodine]({{< relref "/flavours/iodine" >}}), antiseptic and tar.

[Yoichi]({{< relref "/distilleries/yoichi" >}}) in Hokkaido, burning Japanese peat, lands
somewhere else again — drier, and more mineral.

## Why the ppm number misleads

Boxes quote [phenol ppm]({{< relref "/glossary/phenol-ppm" >}}), and drinkers reasonably read
it as a smokiness rating.

It is not one.

The figure is measured **in the malt**, before distillation has even begun. Most of it never
reaches the bottle, and how much does depends on the distillery's cut points, still shape,
copper contact and maturation.

The clearest demonstration of this sits two miles apart on Islay's south coast:

| | [Ardbeg]({{< relref "/distilleries/ardbeg" >}}) | [Lagavulin]({{< relref "/distilleries/lagavulin" >}}) |
|---|---|---|
| Malt specification | ~55 ppm | ~35 ppm |
| Still regime | Purifier, cleaner spirit | Very slow distillation |
| Cask emphasis | Ex-bourbon | Significant [sherry]({{< relref "/flavours/sherry" >}}) |
| Perceived weight | Bright, sharp, citric | Dense, sweet, weighty |

The more heavily peated malt produces the lighter-tasting whisky.

## Where to start

For peat as **attack** — bright, ashy, immediate — try
[Ardbeg 10 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/ardbeg-10-year-old" >}}).

For peat as **depth** — smoke folded into sweetness across sixteen years in wood — try
[Lagavulin 16 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/lagavulin-16-year-old" >}}).

For a non-Scottish reference point,
[Yoichi Single Malt]({{< relref "/whiskies/yoichi-single-malt" >}}).
