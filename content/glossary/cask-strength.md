**Cask strength** whisky is bottled without dilution, at whatever strength it reached in the
cask — typically 50–60% ABV, occasionally higher.

## Why strength varies

Spirit enters the cask at around 63.5% ABV. Over years of maturation it loses both alcohol
and water to evaporation, and the ratio depends on the warehouse: cool damp conditions lose
proportionally more alcohol, warm dry conditions more water. Two identical casks in
different warehouses will finish at different strengths.

## What it implies

Cask strength bottlings are almost always **non-chill-filtered** and of natural colour,
because filtration exists to prevent haze at low strength and haze does not form readily
above roughly 46%. Bottling undiluted therefore tends to bring the other two disclosures
with it — as it does for
[Ardbeg 10 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/ardbeg-10-year-old" >}}), which is not cask
strength but shares the reasoning at 46%.

## Reading the number

A cask strength label usually carries a batch code, because no two batches finish at the
same strength. This is the opposite of the approach taken by a standard
[single malt]({{< relref "/glossary/single-malt" >}}) such as
[Macallan Double Cask 12]({{< relref "/whiskies/macallan-double-cask-12-year-old" >}}), where
consistency is the point and water is added to hit exactly 40% every time.

Neither is better. One optimises for repeatability, the other for showing you the cask.

Adding water at the glass is expected, not a compromise. It lets you find the strength that
suits the whisky rather than accepting the producer's choice.

Cask strength is also the format in which heavily peated whisky is most revealing: at
full strength the [peat smoke]({{< relref "/flavours/peat-smoke" >}}) of an
[Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) malt arrives without the softening that dilution
brings.
