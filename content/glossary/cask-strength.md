**Cask strength** whisky is bottled without dilution, at whatever strength it happened to
reach in the cask — typically 50–60% ABV, and occasionally higher.

## Why strength varies

Spirit goes into the cask at around 63.5% ABV. Then it waits.

Across the years that follow it loses both alcohol and water to evaporation, and which of
the two it loses faster depends entirely on the building around it. Cool, damp conditions
take proportionally more alcohol. Warm, dry conditions take more water. Two identical casks,
filled on the same day and stored in different warehouses, will end their lives at different
strengths.

## What it implies

Cask strength bottlings are almost always **non-chill-filtered** and of natural colour. The
reason is mechanical rather than philosophical: filtration exists to prevent haze at low
strength, and haze does not readily form above roughly 46%. Bottling undiluted therefore
tends to bring the other two disclosures along with it — as it does for
[Ardbeg 10 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/ardbeg-10-year-old" >}}), which is not cask
strength at all, and still arrives at the same reasoning at 46%.

## Reading the number

A cask strength label usually carries a batch code, because no two batches finish at the
same strength. That is the precise opposite of the approach taken by a standard
[single malt]({{< relref "/glossary/single-malt" >}}) such as
[Macallan Double Cask 12]({{< relref "/whiskies/macallan-double-cask-12-year-old" >}}), where
consistency is the whole point and water is added to hit exactly 40%, every time.

Neither is the better idea. One optimises for repeatability. The other for showing you the
cask.

Adding water at the glass is expected, not a compromise. It lets the strength be found
rather than accepted.

Cask strength is also the format in which heavily peated whisky gives most away: at full
strength the [peat smoke]({{< relref "/flavours/peat-smoke" >}}) of an
[Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) malt arrives without any of the softening that
dilution brings.
