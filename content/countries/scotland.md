Scotch whisky is one of the most tightly defined spirits in the world. The Scotch Whisky
Regulations 2009 require that it be produced at a distillery in Scotland from water and
malted barley, matured in oak casks of no more than 700 litres for **at least three
years**, and bottled at no less than **40% ABV**. Nothing may be added except water and
plain caramel colouring.

That last clause matters. Caramel colouring (E150a) is permitted and widely used, which is
why bottle colour tells you almost nothing about cask influence — a point worth
remembering when a deep mahogany whisky is presented as evidence of long sherry
maturation.

## The five regions

The same regulations protect five regional indications: **Highland**, **Lowland**,
**Speyside**, **Campbeltown** and **Islay**. These are geographic boundaries, not flavour
guarantees. [Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) is the closest a region comes to
implying a house style, and even there the correlation is imperfect — Bruichladdich and
Bunnahabhain both sit on Islay and both release largely unpeated whisky.

[Speyside]({{< relref "/regions/speyside" >}}) holds by far the greatest concentration of
distilleries, and the [Highland]({{< relref "/regions/highland" >}}) region is
geographically the largest and least stylistically coherent of the five.

## Malt, grain and blends

Most Scotch sold is **blended** — malt whisky married with lighter grain whisky from column
stills. A [single malt]({{< relref "/glossary/single-malt" >}}) is the product of one distillery from
malted barley alone, and the word "single" refers to that distillery rather than to a single
cask.

Strength is the other axis the label understates: the 40% legal floor and a
[cask strength]({{< relref "/glossary/cask-strength" >}}) bottling are the same category of drink at
very different intensities.

Treat region as a starting point for exploration and
[flavour]({{< relref "/flavours/peat-smoke" >}}) as the thing you actually navigate by. If smoke is
what brought you here, start with
