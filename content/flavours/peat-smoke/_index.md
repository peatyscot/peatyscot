Peat smoke is the most polarising flavour in whisky and the one this site is named for.

## Where it comes from

Barley must be dried before it can be milled. Burn peat under the drying floor and the
smoke passes through the damp grain, depositing phenolic compounds that survive
fermentation, distillation and years in oak.

The flavour is therefore introduced **before distillation ever begins**. It is not a cask
effect and it cannot be added later.

## How it is measured, and why that misleads

Intensity is quoted as [phenol ppm]({{< relref "/glossary/phenol-ppm" >}}), measured in the
malted barley rather than the finished whisky. Much of that phenol content is lost along the
way, and how much is lost depends on the distillery's cut points, still shape and
maturation.

[Ardbeg]({{< relref "/distilleries/ardbeg" >}}) specifies malt at roughly 55 ppm and
[Lagavulin]({{< relref "/distilleries/lagavulin" >}}) at roughly 35 ppm, yet Lagavulin
tastes the heavier of the two. Ppm measures the input, not the experience.

## What it tastes like

Not one flavour but a family, and which member you get depends on what grew in the bog.
Coastal [Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) peat gives the medicinal register —
[iodine]({{< relref "/flavours/iodine" >}}), TCP, seaweed, tar. Inland peat gives something
woodier and more like a garden bonfire. Japanese peat at
[Yoichi]({{< relref "/distilleries/yoichi" >}}) reads drier and more mineral again.

Start with [Ardbeg 10 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/ardbeg-10-year-old" >}}) for peat as
attack, or [Lagavulin 16 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/lagavulin-16-year-old" >}}) for
peat as depth.
