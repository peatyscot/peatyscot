Peat smoke is the most polarising flavour in whisky, and the one this site is named for.

## Where it comes from

Barley must be dried before it can be milled. Burn peat beneath the drying floor and the
smoke passes up through the damp grain, depositing phenolic compounds that will go on to
survive fermentation, distillation, and years in oak.

The flavour is therefore settled **before distillation has begun**. It is not a cask effect,
and it cannot be added afterwards.

## How it is measured, and why that misleads

Intensity is quoted as [phenol ppm]({{< relref "/glossary/phenol-ppm" >}}), measured in the
malted barley rather than in the finished whisky. Much of that content is lost along the
way, and how much is lost depends on the distillery's cut points, still shape and
maturation.

[Ardbeg]({{< relref "/distilleries/ardbeg" >}}) specifies malt at roughly 55 ppm.
[Lagavulin]({{< relref "/distilleries/lagavulin" >}}) at roughly 35 ppm. And yet Lagavulin is
the heavier of the two. Ppm measures the input, never the outcome.

## What it tastes like

Not one flavour, but a family — and which member arrives depends entirely on what grew in
the bog.

Coastal [Islay]({{< relref "/regions/islay" >}}) peat gives the medicinal register:
[iodine]({{< relref "/flavours/iodine" >}}), TCP, seaweed, tar. Inland peat gives something
woodier, closer to a garden bonfire. Japanese peat at
[Yoichi]({{< relref "/distilleries/yoichi" >}}) reads drier and more mineral again.

Start with [Ardbeg 10 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/ardbeg-10-year-old" >}}) for peat as
attack, or [Lagavulin 16 Year Old]({{< relref "/whiskies/lagavulin-16-year-old" >}}) for peat
as depth.
